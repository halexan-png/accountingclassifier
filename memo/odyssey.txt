ODYSSEY
What the classifier does, in the time it takes to read this

Two workbooks arrive, and become one list of rows. Before anything else
touches that list, the rows describing an acquisition or a sale are
pulled out and read on their own — they build a running vocabulary of
this quarter's deals, a list that keeps growing quarter over quarter
rather than starting fresh each time. Every other row is then handed one
of three verdicts: recurring, non-recurring, or a case for a person to
weigh in on.

How a verdict gets made is worth knowing, because it's the difference
between a tool you check and a tool you trust. A written rulebook decides
by default — a fixed set of questions, answered in order, the same way on
every row of every run. The model reads the row's own text, and the
invoice attached to it if there is one, since a deal's name sometimes sits
in the invoice and nowhere else. Recognizing a name from the running deal
list only ever raises a suspicion, never settles anything by itself. And
anything you've told it in writing for this specific quarter — a note
about a disposition, a vendor to watch — outranks all of that, because
it's the one thing in the room the model has no way of knowing on its
own.

Invoices are read one of two ways. A row with a link uses that link; a
row without one is searched, description first, for something that looks
like an invoice number. Worth knowing up front: an invoice longer than
twenty pages is only ever read at its edges — the first two pages and the
last two — so a detail sitting in the middle of a long document can go
unseen.

The single fact most worth carrying out of this page: a run that stops
does not resume. Whatever finished stays finished, but completing the
quarter after an interruption means running it again from the start — and
running it again means paying again, including for rows already done the
first time. Everything else about how spending is capped, and what a Stop
button does versus a Cancel button, follows from that one fact; none of
it changes it.

What comes back at the end is a single file: every row, with its verdict,
the reasoning behind it, and the evidence it leaned on, sitting alongside
the original data. A second sheet collects just the rows worth a person's
attention. A third shows the deal vocabulary that shaped every
M&A-adjacent decision. A fourth totals the quarter.

That's the whole shape of it. The exact meaning of every column and flag,
the precise rules for writing an invoice number, and the fuller account of
everything only touched on here — all of it is one level down, in plutos
gneseos, for whenever a question runs deeper than five minutes can
answer.
